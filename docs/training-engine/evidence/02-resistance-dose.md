# Evidence Base 02: Resistance-Training Dose

Scope: adults 18+, general fitness, home or gym. Compiled 2026-10-13.
Evidence grades: **A** = position stand or guideline; **B** = systematic review or meta-analysis (2016–2026); **C** = practitioner consensus, narrative review, single trial, or engineering assumption.

**Headline:** The newest anchor is the **ACSM 2026 Position Stand**, an overview of reviews (Currier et al., MSSE 58(4):851–872). It replaces the 2009 progression-models stand. Its core messages:
- Train all major muscle groups at least 2 days per week.
- Do 10 or more sets per muscle per week for hypertrophy.
- Do 2–3 sets per exercise per session at 80% 1RM or more for strength.
- Training to failure is not necessary.
- A minimal dose works, and adherence is the top priority.

---

## 1. Weekly volume (sets per muscle group per week)

### Key conclusions

- **More sets means more hypertrophy, with diminishing returns.**
  - Schoenfeld 2017 found about 0.37% extra muscle gain per added weekly set.
  - Mean gains by weekly sets were 5.4% (<5 sets), 6.6% (5–9 sets) and 9.8% (10+ sets).
  - The overall trend was not significant (P = 0.074), and it was sensitive to removing one study. **B**
- **Pelland et al. (2024 preprint; Sports Med 2026) is the largest dose-response analysis:** 67 studies and 2,058 participants.
  - Volume was counted as "fractional sets": direct sets count 1, indirect (synergist) sets count 0.5. This method fit the data best.
  - Hypertrophy: about +0.24% per weekly set, square-root shape.
  - Minimum effective dose for hypertrophy was about 4 fractional sets per week.
  - The number of extra sets needed for each further detectable gain grows with volume: about 6 sets in the 5–10 range, 8.5 in 11–18, 10.75 in 19–29 and 12.5 in 30–42. **B**
  - Strength plateaued much faster. The minimum effective dose was about 1 set per week, and beyond about 5 weekly sets more sets did not reliably add a detectable gain. **B**
- **Per-session cap (Remmert et al. 2025, preprint):**
  - Hypertrophy stopped showing detectable benefit at about 11 fractional sets per muscle per session.
  - Strength stopped at about 2 direct sets per session.
  - These are not hard ceilings, because data above 10 sets per session are sparse. **C** (not yet peer reviewed)
- **Trained men:** Baz-Valle 2022 proposed 12–20 weekly sets per muscle as a practical optimum (young men with 1+ year of training). 12–20 and >20 sets did not differ for quadriceps or biceps; triceps favored >20. **B**
- **ACSM 2026:**
  - Hypertrophy: 10 or more sets per muscle per week, dose-response, plateauing around 18–20 sets.
  - Strength: 2–3 sets per exercise per session, and at least 2 sets per exercise for healthy adults. **A**
- **Minimum effective dose for beginners and general health:**
  - Any regular resistance training beats none. Guidelines ask for all major muscle groups on 2 or more days per week. **A**
  - Iversen 2021 recommends at least 4 weekly sets per muscle in the 6–15 RM range, built on multi-joint lifts. **C** (narrative review)
  - Pelland's minimum effective dose estimate of about 4 fractional sets agrees with this. **B**

### Engine implications

- **Volume unit:** count weekly *fractional* sets per muscle (direct = 1.0, synergist = 0.5).
  - Example: a bench press set gives chest 1.0, triceps 0.5 and front delts 0.5.
- **Defaults by training status (fractional sets per muscle per week):**

| Status | Floor (MED) | Default | Ceiling (engine max) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner (<6 mo) | 4 | 6–10 | 12 |
| Intermediate (6 mo–2 y) | 6 | 10–16 | 20 |
| Advanced (>2 y, opt-in) | 8 | 12–20 | 25–30 |

- **Strength goal:** 2–3 hard sets per main lift per session, 2 or more sessions per week. After about 5 weekly sets per lift, put extra volume toward hypertrophy or other work.
- **Per-session cap:** about 10–11 fractional sets per muscle per session. Spread anything above that across more days.
- **Progression:** add 1–2 sets per muscle per week per mesocycle (4–6 weeks), up to the ceiling. Return to the default after a deload.
  - This rule is an engineering choice (C) that follows the diminishing-returns curve.

### Disagreements and uncertainty

- Training status does not clearly moderate the volume response. ACSM 2026 says experience had minimal impact, but data on trained people are sparse. Treat the higher ceilings for advanced users as tentative.
- Whether to count indirect sets is still debated. Fractional counting fit best for hypertrophy, but direct counting fit best for per-session strength (Remmert).
- Most data are short-term (6–12 weeks) and from young men.

## 2. Training frequency per muscle per week

### Key conclusions

- **Hypertrophy, volume equated:**
  - Schoenfeld 2019 (25 studies) found no significant difference between higher and lower frequency, so frequency can follow preference. **B**
  - Pelland: +0.32% per extra session per week, credible interval crossing zero, which is compatible with a negligible effect. **B**
- **Older evidence:** Schoenfeld 2016 (10 studies) favored training each muscle 2 times per week over once (ES 0.49 vs 0.30). It was superseded by the 2019 update. **B**
- **Strength:**
  - Pelland: about +3.27% per extra weekly session, with diminishing returns. Predicted gains were 12.7% at 1 session per week and 17.3% at 2. **B**
  - Under volume equation, the frequency effect largely disappears.
    - Ralston 2018: volume-equated effect size 0.03, not significant.
    - Grgic 2018 reported higher frequency helps mainly because it allows more volume. [Findings recalled from memory; abstract not opened, citation confirmed]
  - Ralston found a significant upper-body advantage for 3+ vs 1 day per week (ES 0.48). **B**
- **ACSM 2026:** at least 2 sessions per week for strength and for all major muscle groups. Frequency does not affect hypertrophy when volume is equated. **A**

### Engine implications

- **Default:** each muscle 2 times per week; this is the floor for "complete" programming. 1 time per week is allowed if the user only has 1–2 days, keeping volume as high as the time allows.
- **Bounds:** 1–4 exposures per muscle per week.
- **Use frequency to spread volume.** When weekly target ÷ per-session cap (about 10) is above 1, add an exposure.
- **Split templates by days per week:**
  - 1–3 days: full body.
  - 4 days: upper/lower ×2.
  - 5–6 days: upper/lower plus full body, or push/pull/legs.

### Disagreements and uncertainty

- The small strength benefit of frequency may partly be skill practice on the tested lift.
- Very high frequencies (5–6 times per week per muscle) are under-studied.

## 3. Load and repetition ranges

### Key conclusions

- **Hypertrophy is largely load-independent when sets are taken to or near failure.**
  - Schoenfeld 2017 (21 studies; light defined as ≤60% 1RM): muscle gains were 8.3% with heavy loads vs 7.0% with light, not significant. **B**
  - Lopez 2021 network meta-analysis (28 studies, 747 adults): no hypertrophy difference among high (≤8 RM), moderate (9–15 RM) and low (>15 RM) loads. **B**
  - ACSM 2026: loads of 30–100% 1RM give similar hypertrophy. **A**
- **Strength is load-dependent.**
  - Schoenfeld 2017: 1RM gains were 35.4% heavy vs 28.0% light. **B**
  - Lopez 2021: high vs low load SMD about 0.6; moderate vs low about 0.35; high vs moderate 0.26, not significant. **B**
  - ACSM 2026: 80% 1RM or more for strength, with a dose-response. **A**
- **Power (ACSM 2026):** 30–70% 1RM, under about 24 total reps per exercise, with maximal intended concentric speed. **A**
- **ACSM 2009 progression models** (historical, superseded in 2026):
  - Novice: 60–70% 1RM for 8–12 reps, 1–3 sets.
  - Intermediate: same rep range, now with multiple sets.
  - Advanced: 80–100% 1RM periodized (strength), or 70–100% 1RM, mostly 6–12 RM (hypertrophy).
  - Endurance: 10–25+ reps.
  - Load progression: add 2–10% when the user exceeds target reps by 1–2 on two consecutive sessions. **A** (historical)
- **Practical range:** Iversen 2021 suggests 6–15 RM, with 15–40 reps acceptable if taken to failure. **C**

### Engine implications

- **Default rep range by goal:**
  - General fitness or hypertrophy: 6–15 reps.
  - Home or bodyweight: up to 30 reps is acceptable.
  - Strength: 3–6 reps (about 80–90% 1RM) on 1–2 main lifts, plus 6–12 reps on accessories.
- **Beginners:** start at 8–15 reps to learn technique; introduce sets of 6 reps or fewer only after 8–12 weeks.
- **Hard bounds:**
  - Minimum 3 reps for intermediates (1–2 rep singles only for advanced opt-in).
  - Maximum 30 reps; above 30, accuracy of judging RIR falls and sessions take longer.
- **Load progression (double progression):** when the top of the rep range is hit at the target RIR on all sets, add load.
  - Lower-body compounds: +5%, minimum +2.5 kg.
  - Upper-body or small muscles: +2.5%, minimum +1–2 kg.
  - Home users with fixed weights: progress reps, then tempo or harder variations.

### Disagreements and uncertainty

- Very light loads (<30% 1RM) are less studied. Light-load sets need effort close to failure to work, which needs a higher effort level and more discomfort.

## 4. Proximity to failure (RIR) and autoregulation

### Key conclusions

- **Failure is not required.**
  - Grgic 2021/22 (15 studies): no difference for strength (ES −0.09) or hypertrophy (ES 0.22).
  - Non-failure training favored strength when volume was not equated.
  - There was a small hypertrophy edge for failure in trained people (ES 0.15). **B**
- **Refalo 2022/23 (15 studies):** a trivial hypertrophy advantage for set failure (ES 0.19). Momentary failure was no better than non-failure. The authors propose a non-linear relationship. **B**
- **Robinson 2024 meta-regressions:** hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure. Strength is barely affected by RIR and depends more on load (%1RM). How RIR was estimated limits precision. **B**
- **Refalo 2024 RCT (trained, within-subject):** 0 RIR and 1–2 RIR produced near-identical quadriceps growth. Failure caused more fatigue (velocity and rep loss). **C** (single trial)
- **ACSM 2026:** failure is not necessary and may be inadvisable for some, such as older adults. It suggests near-failure or about 2–3 RIR. Evidence is too thin to set exact RIR targets. **A**
- **RIR-based RPE scale:**
  - RPE 10 = 0 RIR, 9 = 1 RIR, and so on (Zourdos 2016). RPE correlated strongly and inversely with bar velocity (r = −0.88 experienced, −0.77 novice).
  - Helms 2016 describes using the scale to adjust load set by set. **C**
- **RIR prediction accuracy:**
  - People underpredict reps to failure by about 1 rep on average (Halperin 2021).
  - Accuracy is better for sets of 12 reps or fewer and closer to failure.
  - Training experience had no meaningful effect.
  - Individual error SD is about 1.5 reps. **B**

### Engine implications

- **Default target RIR:**
  - Beginners: 2–3 RIR (RPE 7–8).
  - Intermediates: 1–2 RIR on most sets.
  - Last set of isolation or machine work: 0–1 RIR allowed.
  - Heavy barbell compounds: never 0 RIR. Keep 1–3 RIR (C/A).
- **Bounds:** RIR 0–4. Sets at 5+ RIR count as warm-up, not working sets.
  - Optional rule: count sets at 4 RIR as 0.5 of a hard set (engine assumption, C).
- **Autoregulation rule (C):** after each set, the user logs RIR or RPE.

| Logged RIR vs target | Load change for the next set or session |
|---|---|
| ≥2 above target (too easy) | +5% |
| 1 above target | +2.5% |
| 1 below target | −2.5% |
| ≥2 below target | −5 to −10% |

- Use reps to failure ≤12 for RIR-guided sets where possible, since accuracy is better there.
- **Calibration aid:** in weeks 1–4, prompt a beginner to take one safe isolation exercise to technical failure, to calibrate their RIR judgement.

### Disagreements and uncertainty

- Robinson and Refalo suggest hypertrophy benefits continue down to about 0–1 RIR, but the size of the gain is uncertain. The fatigue cost suggests 1–2 RIR is the better trade-off for consumers.

## 5. Inter-set rest, supersets and circuits

### Key conclusions

- **Hypertrophy and rest:**
  - Singer 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis (9 studies): rest longer than 60 s may give a small benefit. No appreciable difference beyond 90 s. Effects were small and uncertain. **B**
  - Grgic 2017 systematic review: both short (≤60 s) and long rest work, with a possible edge for long rest in trained people. **B**
- **Strength and rest (Grgic 2018):**
  - Untrained: 60–120 s is sufficient.
  - Trained: more than 2 min helps maximize strength.
  - Under 60 s still gives meaningful gains. **B**
- **ACSM 2026:** short (<1 min) vs long (>1 min) rest did not affect strength; hypertrophy data were insufficient. **A**
  - ACSM 2009 suggested 2–3 min for heavy compounds and 1–2 min for assistance work. **A** (historical)
- **Supersets:**
  - Zhang et al. 2025 meta-analysis (19 studies): shorter sessions, volume load unchanged (SMD 0.05), higher efficiency (SMD 1.74). Chronic strength and hypertrophy did not differ from traditional sets. **B**
  - Agonist-antagonist pairs allowed *more* reps. Same-muscle pairs reduced volume load (SMD −1.08). **B**
  - Paz 2017 trial: an agonist-antagonist session took 8.5 min vs 16 min with traditional sets, with higher volume load. **C**
- **Time-saving methods (Iversen 2021):** supersets, drop sets and rest-pause roughly halve session time at the same volume, and may suit hypertrophy more than maximal strength. **C**
- **Krzysztofik 2019:** agonist-antagonist and upper-lower supersets, drop sets and similar methods may add stimulus and save time. Evidence is not enough for exact dosing. **B** (systematic review, low certainty)
- **Circuit training (ACSM 2026):** improves strength and hypertrophy compared with no exercise. **A**

### Engine implications

- **Default rest:**

| Exercise type | Default rest | Bounds |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy compound (≤6 reps) | 120–180 s | 90–240 s |
| Other compound | 90–120 s | 60–180 s |
| Isolation | 60–90 s | 45–120 s |
| Trained user, strength block | ≥150 s on main lifts | — |

- **Time-constrained sessions:** use agonist-antagonist pairs or upper/lower pairs, such as push+pull or squat+press.
  - Rest about 60–90 s after each pair, so each muscle gets about 2–3 min.
  - Avoid same-muscle supersets except as finishers.
- **Circuits** of 3–4 non-competing exercises for sessions of 20 min or less.
- **Drop sets and rest-pause:** allowed for intermediates on isolation or machine exercises. Count one extended set as about 1.5–2 sets (assumption, C). Do not use on barbell compounds.

### Disagreements and uncertainty

- The hypertrophy benefit of longer rest is small and depends on the muscle studied.
- For general fitness, adherence and session length matter more than rest optimization.

## 6. Time per rep and set, for estimating session length

### Key conclusions

- **Tempo has little effect on outcomes.**
  - Schoenfeld 2015 meta-analysis: similar hypertrophy for rep durations of about 0.5–8 s, when sets go to failure.
  - Super-slow reps (10 s or more) are inferior. **B**
  - ACSM 2026: fast vs moderate-slow contractions did not affect strength; time under tension had no consistent effect. **A**
- **Typical tempos:**
  - ACSM 2009 (via Iversen 2021): about 2 s concentric / 4 s eccentric for novices; 1–2 s / 1–2 s for intermediates. **A/C**
  - Research protocols commonly use 2:0:2, about 4 s per rep. A 10-rep set lasts about 40 s (Paz 2017; De Souza Bezerra 2025). **C**
- **Transitions:** Paz 2017 measured about 10 s to switch between two paired machine exercises. No systematic data exist for setup time between exercises. Practitioner norms are about 60–120 s for free weights and plate loading. **C** (assumption)
- **Warm-up:** Iversen 2021 advises limiting warm-up to exercise-specific ramp sets. ACSM 2009 assumed traditional sessions often exceed 1 hour once warm-up and stretching are included. **C**

### Engine implications: duration model (defaults, C)

**Inputs and default values:**

| Parameter | Default |
|---|---|
| Seconds per rep | 3.5 s (bodyweight/machines 3 s; heavy barbell 4 s; slow eccentric 5 s) |
| Set overhead (unrack, brace, re-rack) | +10 s per set |
| Rest | per table in section 5 |
| Exercise change | 60 s (machines/dumbbells); 120 s (barbell setup); 15 s within a superset |
| Ramp/warm-up sets for first compound | 1–2 sets, about 2–4 min |
| General warm-up | 0 min (≤20-min sessions); 3–5 min (≤45 min); 5–8 min (≥60 min) |

**Set duration:** `set_time = reps × sec_per_rep + 10`, about 40–55 s for 8–12 reps.

**Typical time per working set:**
- Straight sets: about 2.0–2.5 min, including rest.
- Superset pair: about 1.3–1.6 min per set.
- Circuit: about 1.0–1.2 min per set.

**Session templates (working sets are total hard sets, all muscles):**

| Session | Warm-up | Structure | Working sets | Exercises | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 min | ramp only (~1 min) | 2–3 exercise circuit or 1 superset pair, 60 s rest per round | 5–7 | 2–3 | Full body (squat/hinge + push + pull); 1–2 RIR; ×3/wk ≈ 4–6 fractional sets per muscle (MED) |
| 20 min | 2 min | 2 superset pairs | 9–12 | 4 | Full body; meets beginner floor at 2–3×/wk |
| 30 min | 3 min | 2–3 pairs, or straight sets with 60–90 s rest | 12–16 (pairs) / 10–12 (straight) | 4–6 | Default session for beginners |
| 45 min | 4–5 min | Main lift straight sets + 2–3 pairs | 16–20 | 5–7 | Intermediate default; main lift 3 sets × 3–8 reps, 2–3 min rest |
| 60 min | 5–8 min | 1–2 straight-set compounds + pairs | 20–26 | 6–8 | Split or full body; per-muscle cap ~10 fractional sets per session |
| 90 min | 8–10 min | Straight sets, full rest | 28–34 | 8–10 | Advanced/opt-in only; above ~30 sets, warn of diminishing returns |

**Fitting algorithm (C):**
1. Subtract warm-up time.
2. Allocate sets to compound lifts first.
3. Fill with pairs until time runs out.
4. When the weekly per-muscle target cannot fit, apply these in order:
   1. Add a session per week.
   2. Convert straight sets to pairs.
   3. Shorten isolation rest to 60 s.
   4. Accept the lower volume. Anything at or above the floor (4 sets per muscle per week) is acceptable.
5. Always re-estimate session time from the user's logged set and rest times after 2–3 sessions.

### Disagreements and uncertainty

- No peer-reviewed dataset gives typical transition or setup times. Treat all overhead values as calibration defaults that are learned per user.
